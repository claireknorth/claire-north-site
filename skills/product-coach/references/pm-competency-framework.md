## SVPG: The Three Types of Product Coaching

From Marty Cagan's "Becoming a Product Coach" — these map to what a PM needs at different career stages:

### Discovery Coaching (most relevant for mid-level PMs)
Moving from a feature team to an empowered product team primarily means learning how to discover a solution worth building. Teams learning to do product discovery need coaching on:
- Customer interviews and problem validation
- Distinguishing what customers say from what they actually need
- Running experiments (prototypes, spikes, fake doors) before committing to build
- Evidence-based decision making
- "Not everything goes to the sprint"

### Product Leadership Coaching (relevant as the user moves toward senior/staff PM)
Big topics that emerge as PMs grow:
- Product vision — can you articulate a compelling 12-18 month direction?
- Team topology — are you working with the right people on the right things?
- Product strategy — real bets with reasoning, not prioritized backlogs dressed as strategy
- Stakeholder management at senior levels — holding your own with CFO, VP Sales, etc.
- Staffing and developing others

### Transformation Coaching (org-level, less applicable to individual PM growth)
Moving companies from feature-team to empowered product team culture.

## SVPG: Coaching vs. Contracting Distinction

Cagan's key insight: **There's a big difference between doing a thing vs. coaching someone on how to do that thing.**

Implications for self-coaching:
- Don't just ask "what should I build?" — ask "how would I figure out what to build?"
- The goal is to build judgment, not just get the answer
- Pattern recognition comes from experience + reflection, not just doing

## SVPG: What Great PMs Actually Do

From SVPG's model, the job of a product manager has four main responsibilities:

1. **Deep understanding of the customer** — Product managers must be the voice of the customer on the product team. This isn't "user research happens sometimes" — it's a continuous, obsessive practice.

2. **Deep understanding of the data** — Know what the metrics mean, what's moving them, and how to use data to make decisions and build the case for bets.

3. **Deep understanding of the business** — Understand the economics, the constraints, the stakeholders, and the business model. Know why what you're building actually matters to the company.

4. **Deep understanding of the space/technology** — Know the domain well enough to make good trade-offs with engineering and design. This doesn't mean you need to code — but you need technical credibility.

The best PMs are not just strong in one area. Gaps in any of these four create blind spots that cost the team.

## Petra Wille: 5-Step Development Framework

Source: Lenny's Podcast / Wille's work on PM development

**Step 1: Definition of Good**
Before you can develop someone (or yourself), you need a clear, specific definition of what "good" looks like in your context. Not "strong at discovery" but "can run a customer interview that surfaces 2-3 unmet needs within 45 minutes, without leading the witness."

**Step 2: Current Assessment**
Honestly assess where you are today against that standard. Use concrete evidence — not how you feel about the skill, but what you've actually done and how it went.

**Step 3: Shared Vision**
Where do you want to be? What does growth look like? Align on the goal before diving into tactics.

**Step 4: Development Plan**
Specific, time-boxed actions that bridge the gap. The best development plans have real reps with real feedback loops — not just "read about X."

**Step 5: Follow-up**
Track progress and check in. Development without accountability rarely produces results.

## Bangaly Kaba: Bloom's Taxonomy for PM Skill Diagnosis

Use Bloom's Taxonomy to figure out where someone actually is on the skill curve — which tells you what type of development they need:

| Level | What it Looks Like | What They Need |
|---|---|---|
| Knowledge | Can define the concept | Exposure, examples |
| Comprehension | Can explain it to others | Discussion, frameworks |
| Application | Can use it in routine situations | Guided practice reps |
| Analysis | Can diagnose problems, identify patterns | Complex case work, reflection |
| Synthesis | Can combine concepts, adapt to new situations | Stretch projects, mentorship |
| Evaluation | Can judge quality, teach others | Teaching, coaching others |

Most PMs are at Application for their strengths and Knowledge/Comprehension for their gaps. The fix is different at each level — don't prescribe advanced case analysis to someone who needs basic application reps.

## Christian Strunk: Four Coaching Modes

From "Product Coaching: What It Is and Why It Delivers 700% ROI":

| Mode | % of Time | What it Is | When to Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coaching | 70% | Asking powerful questions that help you discover answers you already possess but can't access | When you feel stuck, conflicted, or unclear |
| Mentoring | 15% | Sharing experience and patterns from someone who has been there | When navigating a specific unfamiliar situation |
| Consulting | 10% | Expert analysis and specific recommendations | When facing a specialized decision or structural problem |
| Training | 5% | Structured knowledge delivery when foundational gaps exist | When you literally don't know the concept yet |

For self-coaching: most of the time you need coaching (questions to unlock your own thinking) not consulting (being told what to do). Resist the urge to immediately look for the "right answer."

## Warning Signs You Need Coaching (Strunk)

These are patterns that signal a real capability gap (not just a bad week):

- Repeated pivots without learning why
- Stakeholder conflicts you can't seem to resolve
- Features shipping without clear impact on business outcomes (feature factory)
- Decision paralysis — struggling to prioritize or commit to direction  
- High complexity/low output ratio on your projects
- Feedback you keep receiving that you don't know how to act on

## Common PM Coaching Mistakes to Avoid

From the Lenny coaching-pms skill:

1. **Coaching without a competency model** — Development is aimless without a clear picture of what "good" looks like first
2. **Treating all weaknesses the same** — Junior-level gaps need different fixes than senior-level gaps
3. **Focusing only on weaknesses** — Great development amplifies strengths AND manages around gaps
4. **Skipping the follow-up** — Development plans without accountability loops don't produce results
5. **Expecting insight without doing reps** — You can't read your way to being good at discovery. You need reps.
